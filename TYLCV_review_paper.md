# Tomato Yellow Leaf Curl Virus (TYLCV): Biology, Vector Interactions, Resistance Breeding and Emerging Management Strategies — A Review

**Date:** November 2026

## Abstract

Tomato yellow leaf curl virus (TYLCV; genus *Begomovirus*, family *Geminiviridae*) is among the most damaging pathogens of tomato (*Solanum lycopersicum*) worldwide. It is transmitted in a persistent, circulative manner by the *Bemisia tabaci* cryptic species complex and can cause severe yield loss in unprotected crops. This review summarises recent work (2021–2026) on TYLCV genome function, vector transmission, global spread and recombination, host resistance (Ty genes), vector management and biotechnological interventions including CRISPR/Cas genome editing and RNA interference. Studies are compared across approach, strengths and limitations, and the main research gaps are identified: resistance durability under high disease pressure and heat, incomplete molecular understanding of the virus–vector interface, insecticide resistance in whiteflies, and the limited field validation of genome-edited and RNA-based protection. We conclude that durable control will require pyramiding of Ty loci, vector exclusion, and field-tested editing strategies integrated into a single management framework.

**Keywords:** TYLCV; begomovirus; *Bemisia tabaci*; Ty genes; resistance breakdown; CRISPR/Cas; RNA interference; integrated pest management

## 1. Introduction

Tomato is one of the most widely grown vegetable crops, and its production is constrained by many pathogens. Among them, tomato yellow leaf curl disease (TYLCD) ranks among the most important, with the monopartite begomovirus TYLCV as its best-known causal agent [1]. Typical symptoms are upward leaf curling, marginal chlorosis, severe stunting and flower drop; infection before flowering can cause near-total loss of marketable fruit.

TYLCV was first associated with disease in the Middle East, and phylogeographic analyses place the origin of the first TYLCVs in that region, with later worldwide dissemination largely driven by trade and the spread of invasive whitefly lineages [5]. The disease now occurs across tropical, subtropical and warm-temperate production areas. This review (i) outlines TYLCV biology, (ii) synthesises recent literature on transmission, evolution and management, (iii) compares the main control strategies, and (iv) identifies gaps and future directions.

## 2. Background and Related Work

### 2.1 Taxonomy and genome organisation

TYLCV belongs to the genus *Begomovirus*; the family has recently been reorganised with the establishment of new genera [17], and begomoviruses are widely discussed as a highly successful and adaptable virus group [18]. Virions are twinned (geminate) particles formed by two incomplete icosahedra (~20 × 30 nm) enclosing a circular single-stranded DNA genome of about 2.8 kb. The genome carries six overlapping open reading frames on the virion-sense and complementary-sense strands, separated by an intergenic region containing the origin of replication:

| ORF | Protein | Main function |
|---|---|---|
| V1 | Coat protein (CP) | Encapsidation, systemic movement, vector interaction |
| V2 | Pre-coat protein | Movement; strong silencing suppressor |
| C1 | Replication-associated protein (Rep) | Rolling-circle replication initiation |
| C2 | Transcriptional activator (TrAP) | Transcription control; silencing suppression |
| C3 | Replication enhancer (REn) | Enhances replication |
| C4 | Pathogenicity factor | Symptom induction, movement, silencing suppression |

Comparative work on the silencing suppressors of TYLCD-associated viruses showed that V2 is typically the strongest suppressor, with strain- and host-dependent activity of C2 and C4; this underlies much of the virus's ability to overcome RNA-silencing defences.

### 2.2 Host range

Besides tomato, TYLCV infects other solanaceous crops (pepper, eggplant, tobacco), common bean, and weeds such as *Datura stramonium*, *Solanum nigrum* and *Malva parviflora*, which act as inoculum reservoirs.

### 2.3 Vector and transmission

TYLCV is transmitted by *B. tabaci* (notably MEAM1 and MED) in a persistent, circulative manner: virions are acquired from phloem, cross the midgut into the haemolymph, and reach the salivary glands before inoculation [3]. A whitefly endosymbiont (*Hamiltonella*) GroEL chaperonin has been linked to protecting virions in the haemolymph and to differences in transmission efficiency between whitefly species. Whitefly salivary effectors also modulate plant hormone signalling, generally tipping the balance towards salicylic acid and away from jasmonic acid defences, which can favour both the insect and virus [8].

## 3. Literature Review

### 3.1 Global status and epidemiology

Recent global surveys emphasise that TYLCD now involves multiple virus species and strains and is a threat on every producing continent [1]. A genome-scale analysis of more than 800 TYLCV genomes reported that the Israel (IL) and Mild (Mld) strains are present across Africa, the Americas, Asia, Oceania and Europe, whereas recombinant strains were mainly found in the Middle East [5]. In the western Mediterranean, a survey of field samples collected between 2015 and 2019 found the invasive recombinant TYLCV-IS76 in Morocco but not in Italy, showing country-specific shifts in TYLCV/TYLCSaV recombinant populations [13].

### 3.2 Host resistance and the Ty loci

Six major loci (Ty-1 to Ty-6), introgressed from wild tomato relatives, have been used in breeding [1,2]:

- **Ty-1/Ty-3** are allelic and encode an RNA-dependent RNA polymerase (RDR) that enhances methylation of viral DNA and gives broad geminivirus resistance [2].
- **Ty-2** (chromosome 11, *S. habrochaites*) encodes an NB-LRR protein and is effective against TYLCV but not against all other begomoviruses [2].
- **ty-5** is recessive and results from loss of function of the mRNA-surveillance factor Pelota (*SlPelo*).
- **Ty-6** (chromosome 10) was recently identified as a variant of DNA polymerase delta subunit 1 (SlPOLD1) [6]; combined with Ty-3 or ty-5 it gives the highest resistance levels.
- **Ty-4** is a minor-effect locus.

Resistance is not absolute. Virus accumulation can still be detected in resistant plants, and breakdown of Ty-1-mediated resistance has been reported in Spain, Morocco and Italy, linked to TYLCV/TYLCSV recombinants and to high inoculum pressure [2,7]. Ty-1/Ty-3 activity is also reported to decline at higher temperatures, whereas Ty-2 inhibition is more stable [7].

### 3.3 Vector management

Whitefly control combines exclusion (fine-mesh nets), reflective mulches, trapping and insecticides. Insecticide resistance is a growing problem: surveys of *B. tabaci* populations on vegetable crops report resistance mutations across multiple chemical classes and cite hundreds of resistance cases globally [10]. Recent field trials show that rotations of synthetic insecticides still cut whitefly numbers more consistently than biopesticides [15], which argues for integrated management and resistance management rather than reliance on a single tool. Biological control with predators, parasitoids and entomopathogens is increasingly reviewed as a component of IPM [11], and combining resistant cultivars with exclusion has been associated with lower insecticide use and better biological control.

### 3.4 Biotechnological approaches

*CRISPR/Cas.* Two strategies dominate: targeting the viral genome, and editing host susceptibility genes. Editing *SlPelo* in tomato restricted viral DNA accumulation and gave TYLCV resistance [4]. Direct targeting of viral genomes has produced heritable interference, but targeting coding regions can generate escape variants (reported in an earlier preprint, not included in this 2021+ list). CRISPR-mediated resistance has since been extended to related begomoviruses such as ToLCNDV [16].

*RNA interference.* Topically applied dsRNA from TYLCV C4 and V2 moved systemically in tomato and persisted for weeks [14], yet a controlled comparison reported no significant reduction in TYLCV-IL accumulation from external dsRNA, whereas a hairpin (hpRep) construct conferred strong resistance [9]. Nanoformulated dsRNA targeting both whitefly and virus is under development.

## 4. Comparative Analysis of Existing Research

| Strategy | Representative evidence | Strengths | Limitations |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ty-1/Ty-3 breeding | [2,7] | Broad geminivirus resistance; widely deployed | Breakdown under high pressure/recombinants; weaker at high temperature |
| Ty-2 | [2,7] | Stable effect at higher temperature | Narrower virus spectrum |
| ty-5 (Pelota) / Ty-6 (POLD1) | [4,6] | New mechanisms; best when stacked | Few multi-environment durability data; Ty-6 gene identified only recently |
| CRISPR (host gene editing) | [4] | Non-transgenic potential; strong restriction of viral DNA | Regulation; pleiotropic effects of editing susceptibility genes |
| CRISPR (viral targeting) | [4,16] | Heritable virus interference | Escape variants; delivery |
| Topical dsRNA / RNAi | [9,14] | Non-transgenic, quick | Inconsistent protection; stability; cost |
| Insecticides | [10,15] | Rapid vector suppression | Resistance, environmental load |
| Biological control | [11] | Sustainable, compatible with IPM | Variable efficacy; virus transmission occurs after very short feeding |
| Exclusion/mulches | [3] | No chemical input | Cost; ventilation; short protective window |

## 5. Research Gaps

1. **Durability of resistance.** Few studies evaluate stacked Ty loci across seasons, temperatures and virus populations, even though recombinants are known to overcome Ty-1 [2,7,13].
2. **Virus–vector interface.** Candidate whitefly proteins and effectors have been described, but the roles of specific effectors in TYLCV transmission remain incompletely resolved [3,8].
3. **Climate effects.** Temperature affects Ty gene performance and likely modifies vector dynamics; modelling suggests warmer conditions can worsen outbreak persistence [12], but multi-year field data are scarce.
4. **Field validation of editing and RNA tools.** Most CRISPR and dsRNA results come from controlled conditions [4,9,14].
5. **Surveillance.** Recombinant detection is uneven geographically [5,13].

## 6. Challenges and Limitations

- Resistance is partial and virus replication is not fully blocked, allowing selection of adapted variants.
- Insecticide resistance in *B. tabaci* erodes chemical options [10,15].
- Regulatory and public-acceptance barriers affect genome-edited cultivars.
- Cost and infrastructure limit net-house use for smallholders.
- Research is concentrated on a few strains (IL, Mld); data on other regions and recombinants are limited.

## 7. Future Research Directions

- Pyramiding Ty-1/Ty-3, Ty-2, ty-5 and Ty-6 with marker-assisted selection, with multi-location, multi-temperature durability testing [2,6].
- Editing susceptibility genes (*SlPelo*) and multiplexed viral targets, with field trials and off-target assessment [4].
- Dual-target dsRNA/nanocarrier systems against virus and vector.
- Functional studies of whitefly effectors and endosymbiont proteins as targets for blocking transmission [3,8].
- Genomic surveillance for recombinants and early-warning systems [5,13].
- Combining resistant cultivars, exclusion and biocontrol under IPM with insecticide-resistance management [11,15].

## 8. Conclusion

TYLCV remains a major constraint on tomato production because of its efficient whitefly transmission, rapid evolution and recombination, and the incomplete durability of single resistance sources. Recent identification of new resistance mechanisms (Pelota, POLD1), genome-editing demonstrations and improved IPM knowledge provide a stronger toolkit, but field-validated, multi-layered strategies are still needed. Integrating stacked host resistance, vector exclusion and biological control, supported by genomic surveillance, offers the most realistic route to durable protection.
